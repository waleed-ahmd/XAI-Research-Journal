\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting asks a large language model (LLM) to write
out intermediate reasoning steps before giving its answer. Wei et
al.~\cite{wei2022} showed that this alone, with no extra training, raised
PaLM 540B from 17.9\% to 56.9\% accuracy on the GSM8K maths benchmark. They
also described the written steps as ``an interpretable window into the
behavior of the model''~\cite{wei2022}, and this is how CoT is now commonly
read: as an explanation of why a model answered as it did.

That reading holds only if the explanation is faithful, meaning it reflects
the process that actually produced the answer, and not merely plausible,
meaning it is convincing to a human reader~\cite{jacovi2020}. Turpin et
al.~\cite{turpin2023} showed that the two can come apart. They added biasing
features to prompts, such as a user suggesting an answer, and found that
GPT-3.5 and Claude~1.0 shifted towards the biased answer, with accuracy
falling by up to 36\% across 13 BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) tasks, whilst their
explanations almost never mentioned the bias. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025}
later found the same pattern in reasoning models: Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned
a hint it had used in about 25\% of cases, and DeepSeek R1 in about 39\%.

The models Turpin et al. tested have since been replaced, however, and Chen
et al. used different hints and benchmarks. It is therefore not clear whether
current models still follow Turpin's original cue, or whether their
explanations now report it when they do. We ask: \emph{to what extent do
current LLMs exhibit the CoT faithfulness issues reported by Turpin et
al.~\cite{turpin2023} when evaluated using the same type of controlled cue?}
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a controlled adaptation of Turpin et al.'s Suggested Answer
    experiment on 270 questions from their released BBH
    data~\cite{turpin2023code}, covering all 13 tasks, applied to two current
    models, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol;
  \item an Original Repeat condition that separates answer changes caused by
    the cue from the variation a model shows when asked the same question
    twice; and
  \item blinded coding by two human raters of whether each cue-following
    explanation acknowledges the cue.
\end{itemize}

We find that the cue rarely changes current models' answers (10 of 540 cued
responses), but when it does, the explanation rarely says so: both raters
found that 9 of the 10 explanations justify the cued answer without referring
to the suggestion.

Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews prior work, Section~\ref{sec:method}
describes our method, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and
Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss them and
state the limitations.
