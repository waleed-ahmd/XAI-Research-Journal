\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting encourages a large language model (LLM) to
generate intermediate reasoning steps before producing a final answer,
typically by including worked examples of such reasoning in the prompt. Wei
et al.~\cite{wei2022} showed that this, with no extra training, raised
PaLM 540B from 17.9\% to 56.9\% accuracy on the GSM8K maths benchmark. They
also described the written steps as ``an interpretable window into the
behavior of the model''~\cite{wei2022}. Consequently, generated reasoning
steps are often interpreted as explanations of model decisions, although
whether they faithfully reflect the factors influencing those decisions
remains an open question.

Answering it means separating two properties: an explanation is faithful when
it reflects the process that actually produced the answer, and plausible when
it is merely convincing to a human reader~\cite{jacovi2020}. Turpin et
al.~\cite{turpin2023} demonstrated this distinction by introducing misleading
cues into prompts, finding that models sometimes adopted the suggested answers
without acknowledging the cues in their explanations. Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2025} later found the same pattern in reasoning models: Claude
3.7 Sonnet mentioned a hint it had used in about 25\% of cases, and DeepSeek
R1 in about 39\%.

However, differences in model generations, cue designs and evaluation
benchmarks leave uncertainty about how current LLMs respond to comparable
misleading answer suggestions. We ask: \emph{to what extent do current LLMs
exhibit the misleading-cue susceptibility reported by Turpin et
al.~\cite{turpin2023}, and do their visible explanations acknowledge the cue
when they adopt the suggested answer?} Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a controlled adaptation of Turpin et al.'s Suggested Answer
    experiment on 270 questions from their released BIG-Bench Hard (BBH)
    data~\cite{turpin2023code}, covering all 13 tasks, applied to two current
    models, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol;
  \item an uncued Original Repeat condition that gives a baseline for
    ordinary response variability, helping to distinguish apparent
    cue-following from answer changes that occur when the same question is
    simply asked again, although one repeat per question cannot fully
    separate the two; and
  \item blinded coding by two human raters of whether each cue-following
    explanation acknowledges the cue.
\end{itemize}

Across 540 cue-condition responses (270 per model), we identified ten
cue-following cases, in which the answer switched to the suggested incorrect
option: nine for GPT-5.6 Sol and one for Claude Opus 5.5. Both human raters
classified nine of the ten associated visible explanations as not
acknowledging the misleading suggestion.
