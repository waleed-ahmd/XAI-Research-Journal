\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

An explanation is \emph{faithful} when it correctly reflects
the factors that actually produced a model's prediction, and
\emph{plausible} when it merely reads as a convincing account
of that prediction \cite{turpin2023}. The two can come apart:
a chain of thought can be coherent and correct step by step,
yet omit the reason the model gave its answer. Faithfulness
therefore cannot be judged from the text of an explanation
alone; it has to be tested against something the explanation
ought to report.

Turpin et al.\ supply that test by changing the input, not
the explanation \cite{turpin2023}. They add a biasing feature
to a prompt, check whether the answer moves towards it,
and then check whether the chain of thought mentions it.
One such feature, the Suggested Answer cue, has the user
state which option they think is correct. Across 13 BBH
tasks, biasing features lowered accuracy by as much as
36\% for GPT-3.5 and Claude 1.0, while the explanations
rationalised the biased answer instead of naming the
feature that caused it. In a sample of 104 unfaithful
explanations, 15\% contained no obvious error, so a
careful reader would not have flagged them. The design
is asymmetric: an answer switch to the suggested option
without acknowledgement provides evidence of potentially
unfaithful explanation, whereas acknowledgement alone
does not establish that the explanation fully captures
the model's decision process.

Lanham et al.\ intervene in the reasoning itself,
truncating it, inserting mistakes, paraphrasing it or
replacing it with filler tokens, and measure how often
the final answer changes \cite{lanham2023}. They report
that larger, more capable models produced less faithful
reasoning on most tasks studied. Bentham et al.\ later
reproduced this scaling trend only under specific
conditions and found that, once normalised, the metric
correlates strongly with accuracy ($R^2 = 0.74$), which
questions what it measures \cite{bentham2024}. Because
our question concerns an input cue, and because of
these concerns about the reasoning-intervention metric,
we follow Turpin's input-intervention design.

Two recent studies apply that design to reasoning models.
Chen et al.\ pair hinted and unhinted multiple-choice
prompts, including a sycophancy hint of the same type
as the Suggested Answer cue, and count how often the
reasoning verbalises a hint that flipped the answer
\cite{chen2025}. Among cases where hints influenced the
answer, the average acknowledgement rates across hint
types were approximately 25\% for Claude 3.7 Sonnet
and 39\% for DeepSeek R1. Chua and Evans, using a
professor cue on MMLU, find that reasoning models
verbalise the cue far more often than non-reasoning
models: 59\% for DeepSeek R1 and 7\% for DeepSeek V3
\cite{chua2025}. These findings suggest that reasoning
models can acknowledge influential cues more frequently
in some settings, although explanation unfaithfulness
remains observable.

Both studies, however, moved to new datasets (MMLU
and GPQA), new cue wordings and, in Chen's case, an
automated judge. Both studies examined generated
reasoning traces available from the evaluated models,
while Chen et al.\ could not access the full generated
reasoning traces of OpenAI's o1 and o3. Neither study
directly revisits Turpin et al.'s BBH Suggested Answer
setting with the models evaluated here. We address
this gap through a controlled adaptation incorporating
an uncued repeat condition and human evaluation of
visible explanation acknowledgement.
