\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

An explanation is \emph{faithful} when it reflects correctly the factors that actualy produced a model's prediction, and \emph{plausible} when it merely reads as a convincing account of that prediction \cite{turpin2023}. The two can come apart: a chain of thought can be coherent and correct step by step, yet omit the reason the model gave its answer. Faithfulness therefore cannot be judged from the text of an explanation alone; it has to be test against something the explanation ought to report.

Turpin et al.\ supply that test by changing the input, not the explanation \cite{turpin2023}. They adda  biasing feature to a prompt, check whether the answer moves towards it, and then check whether the chain of throught mentions it. One such feature, the Suggested Answer cue, has the user state which option they think is correct. Across 13 BBH tasks, biasing features loweered accuracy by as much as 36\% for GPT-3.5 and Claude 1.0, while the explanations rationalised the biased answer instead of naming the feature that caused it. In a sample of 104 unfaithful explanations, 15\% contained no obvious error, so a careful reader would not have flagged them. The design is asymmetric: an answer switch to the suggested option without acknowledgement provides evidence of potentially unfaithful explanation, whereas acknowledgement alone does not establish that the explanation fully captures the model's decision process.

Lanham et al.\ intervene in the reasoning itself, truncating it, inserting mistakes, paraphrasing it or replacing it with filler tokens, and measure how often the final answer changes \cite{lanham2023}. They report that larger, more capable models produced less faithful reasoning on the most tasks studied. Bentham et a.\ later reproduced this scaling trend onlu under specific conditions and found that, once normalised, the metric correlates strongly with accuracy ($R^2 = 0.74$), which questions what it measures \cite{bentham2024}. Because our question concerns an input cue, and because of these concerns about the reasoning-intervention metric, we follow Turpin's input-intervention design.

Two recent studies apply that design to reasoning models. Chen et al.\ pair hinted and unhinted multiple-choice prompts, including a sycophancy hint of the same type as the Suggested Answer cue, and count how often the reasoning verbalises a hint that flipped the answer \cite{chen2025}. Among cases where the hint influenced the model's answer, the average acknowledgement rates were approximately 25\% for Claude 3.7 Sonnet and 39\% for DeepSeek R1. Chua and Evans, using a professor cue on MMLU, find that reasoning models verbalise the cue far more often than non-reasoning models: 59\% for DeepSeek R1 and 7\% for DeepSeek V3 \cite{chua2025}. Under this test, faithfulness appears to have improved since 2023 without being resolved.

Both studies, however, moved to new datasets (MMLU and GPQA), new cue wordings and, in Chen's case, an automated judge, and both read the the model's generated reasoning traces, which Chen et al.\ could not obtain for OpenAI's o1 and o3. Neither study revisits Turpin et al's BBH Suggested Answer setting using the current models evaluated here. Our study addresses this narrower gap by combining controlled misleading-answer interventions, an uncued repeat condition, and human coding of visible explanation acknowledgement.
