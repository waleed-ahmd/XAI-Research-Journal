\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Overall Performance and Cue Susceptibility}

(BBH) tasks, producing 1,620 responses from GPT-5.6 Sol and
Claude Opus 5.5 under three conditions: original, misleading
cue, and repeat. Table~\ref{tab:overall_results} summarises

Both models exhibited relatively small reductions in accuracy
under the misleading-cue condition: 1.9 percentage points for
GPT-5.6 Sol and 0.4 percentage points for Claude Opus 5.5.
However, accuracy alone does not capture cue-following
behaviour. GPT produced nine cases (3.3\%) where its answer
changed to the incorrect option suggested by the cue,
compared with one case (0.4\%) for Claude.

\subsection{Baseline Answer Variability}

The repeat condition provides a reference for answer
instability without an injected cue. GPT exhibited seven
answer switches (2.6\%) between the original and repeat
conditions, while Claude exhibited three (1.1\%).
These results demonstrate that answer changes can occur
even without misleading cues, highlighting the importance
of considering ordinary response variability when
interpreting cue-following behaviour.

Across the 13 tasks, GPT's nine cue-following cases occurred
in five task categories, while Claude's single case occurred
in one category. This suggests a non-uniform distribution
within the evaluated sample, but the limited per-task sample
sizes prevent reliable conclusions about task-specific
susceptibility.

\subsection{Visible Explanation Acknowledgement}

The visible explanations associated with the ten raw
cue-following cases were independently classified by two
human raters into three categories: \emph{explicit}
(acknowledges the suggestion's influence),
\emph{mentions} (refers to the suggestion without
attributing influence), and \emph{none}
(does not acknowledge or mention the suggestion).
The raters agreed on all ten cases (100\% raw agreement;
Cohen's $\kappa = 1.00$). All nine GPT-5.6 Sol
explanations received the \emph{none} label, whereas
Claude Opus 5.5's sole explanation received
\emph{explicit}. No explanations were classified as
\emph{mentions}. Claude's result represents only one
case and does not establish a general acknowledgement rate.

For example, in a Sports Understanding question,
GPT-5.6 Sol changed its answer from ``plausible''
to the incorrectly suggested ``implausible''.
Its visible explanation stated that the named player
``would not ordinarily make that strategic decision
himself'', without acknowledging the suggestion.
The uncued repeat returned to ``plausible''.
Both human raters classified the explanation as
\emph{none}.

Together, these results show that cue-following was
uncommon, yet most associated visible explanations did
not acknowledge the suggestion. This does not establish
its influence on hidden reasoning, and the ten coded
cases limit generalisation.
