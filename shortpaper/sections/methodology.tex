\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

We evaluate 270 questions drawn from Big-Bench Hard (BBH). The questions
themselves are not ours: we use the data Turpin et al. released alongside
their original study, rather than writing new items, so our sample stays
directly comparable to theirs \cite{turpin2023,turpin2023code}. The 270
questions span all 13 BBH tasks Turpin used, 30 Temporal Sequences questions
and 20 from each of the other 12, a fixed split drawn under a recorded seed.
Sixty of these questions survive from two earlier pilots: one 30-question run
on a single task, then a second 30-question run spread across all 13. The
remaining 210 were sampled afterwards to fill out the balanced quota. Why the
expansion? Both pilots produced too few cue-induced answer flips on one task
alone to say anything meaningful about acknowledgement. We report that
sequence honestly, rather than presenting the full 270-question design as
though it had been fixed from the outset.

Each question meets each model under three conditions. \textit{Original} is
the bare question. \textit{Cue} adds a ``Suggested Answer'' sentence naming a
wrong option, following Turpin's construction \cite{turpin2023}.
\textit{Original\_repeat} asks the same bare question again, in a fresh call.
Comparing \textit{original} against \textit{cue} shows how far the cue pulls
an answer; comparing \textit{original} against \textit{original\_repeat}
shows how much an answer wobbles with no cue at all. That second comparison
matters: without it, any shift seen under cue could just be noise rather
than the cue's doing. Every question, condition, model and run is answered
once, keyed against its own configuration, so an interrupted collection
picks back up where it stopped instead of re-asking, and re-paying, for work
already done.

Two models are compared: Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol. Each is the current
flagship of its provider, chosen to match by tier rather than by price or
speed. Both run at the same \texttt{medium} reasoning-effort setting, fixed
explicitly in configuration rather than left at whatever each provider
defaults to. Otherwise, the model comparison would really be a comparison of
effort budgets instead.

We rate only the explanation text a model gives as part of its ordinary,
user-visible answer. We do not use any separate internal reasoning or
``thinking'' summary an API might expose. Neither provider ever hands back
the literal chain of thought behind an answer, only a summary or nothing, and
we record which one for every single response rather than assuming. This
caps, honestly, what the study can claim about why a model answered as it
did. Following Chen et al.'s practice of filtering on cases where a hint
actually changed the answer \cite{chen2025}, we restrict acknowledgement
rating to responses where the cue flipped the answer to the suggested, wrong
option. For each of those, two human raters work blind to model identity, on
a shuffled case order, and independently label the explanation as
\emph{explicit}, \emph{mentions}, or \emph{none}: does it, in its own words,
admit the suggestion changed its mind? Agreement between the two raters is
scored with Cohen's kappa. We kept this judgement with humans. Asking a model
to judge another model's faithfulness would hand the question straight back
to the kind of system this study doubts.

This is a controlled adaptation of Turpin et al.'s Suggested Answer
experiment \cite{turpin2023}, not a strict replication. The models are
2026's flagship tier, not 2023's; reasoning effort is fixed to a shared level
neither provider defaults to; and the sample now spans 13 BBH tasks unevenly,
rather than one task in depth. All 1{,}620 responses (270 questions $\times$
three conditions $\times$ two models) were collected by 21 October 2026, with
no failed or missing requests. That figure describes collection completeness
only. Accuracy and explanation faithfulness are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.
