\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

We evaluate 270 questions drawn from Big-Bench Hard (BBH), using the data
Turpin et al. released alongside their original study instead of writing new
items, so our sample stays directly comparable to theirs
\cite{turpin2023,turpin2023code}. The 270 questions span all 13 BBH tasks
Turpin used: 30 Temporal Sequences questions and 20 from each of the other
12, a balanced, fixed split drawn under a recorded seed. Sixty of these
questions survive from two earlier pilots, one 30-question run on a single
task and a second 30-question run spread across all 13. The remaining 210
were sampled afterwards to complete the balanced quota. Both pilots produced
too few cue-induced answer flips on one task alone to support a meaningful
acknowledgement analysis, which is why the sample was expanded. We report
that sequence as it happened, not as though the full 270-question design had
been fixed from the outset.

Each question is presented to each model under three conditions.
\textit{Original} is the bare question. \textit{Cue} adds a ``Suggested
Answer'' sentence naming a wrong option, following Turpin's construction
\cite{turpin2023}. \textit{Original\_repeat} asks the same bare question a
second time, in a separate call: a single repeat per question, giving a thin
baseline for how much an answer varies with no cue present. Comparing
\textit{original} against \textit{cue} shows how far the cue shifts an
answer; comparing \textit{original} against \textit{original\_repeat}
isolates that baseline variation, so a shift under cue is not mistaken for
ordinary noise. Each question, condition, model and run is answered once and
keyed against its own configuration, so an interrupted collection resumes
without re-asking, or re-paying, for completed work.

Two models are compared: Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, each provider's
current flagship model, matched by tier. Both run at the same, explicitly
configured \texttt{medium} reasoning-effort setting, controlling effort as a
variable separate from model choice.

We rate only the explanation text a model gives as part of its ordinary,
user-visible answer, not any separate internal reasoning or ``thinking''
summary an API might expose. Neither provider returns the literal chain of
thought behind an answer, only a summary or nothing; which of the two
applies is recorded for every response. This limits what the study can claim
about why a model answered as it did. Following Chen et al.'s practice of
filtering on cases where a hint changed the answer \cite{chen2025}, we
restrict acknowledgement rating to responses where the cue flipped the
answer to the suggested, wrong option. For each of those, two human raters
work blind to model identity, on a shuffled case order, and independently
label the explanation using one of three categories: \emph{explicit}, where
the explanation states that it is going with the suggested answer or that
the suggestion changed its mind; \emph{mentions}, where the explanation
refers to the suggestion without crediting it as the reason for the final
answer; and \emph{none}, where the explanation does not refer to the
suggestion at all. Agreement between the two raters is scored with Cohen's
kappa. Human raters make this judgement, since model-generated explanations
of influence are precisely what the study evaluates.

This is a controlled adaptation of Turpin et al.'s Suggested Answer
experiment \cite{turpin2023}, not a strict replication: the models are
2026's flagship tier, not 2023's, and reasoning effort is fixed to a shared
level that neither provider defaults to. All 1{,}620 responses (270
questions $\times$ three conditions $\times$ two models) were collected by
21 October 2026, with no failed or missing requests, describing collection
completeness, not answer accuracy or explanation faithfulness, which are
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
