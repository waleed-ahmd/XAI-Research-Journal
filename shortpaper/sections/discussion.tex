\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

Turpin et al.\ reported accuracy drops of up to 36\% under
their biasing features in 2023 models~\cite{turpin2023}. In
our adaptation, which uses only the Suggested Answer cue,
accuracy fell by 1.9 percentage points for GPT-5.6 Sol and
0.4 for Claude Opus 5.5. These drops are smaller, but
differences in cue types, samples and models rule out a
direct quantitative comparison.

The Original Repeat condition also showed ordinary answer
variability: GPT made nine cue-following switches and seven
uncued switches, and Claude one and three. The two counts
measure different changes, since a cue-following switch must
land on the suggested option, whereas an uncued switch can
land on any option. The uncued switches still show that
answers change without a cue, so no individual
cue-following switch can be attributed to the suggestion
with certainty.

The acknowledgement result is clearer. In nine of the ten
cue-following cases, the explanation did not refer to the
suggestion, the pattern Turpin et al.\ reported and in line
with the low acknowledgement rates Chen et al.\ found for
reasoning models~\cite{chen2025}. Cue-following is now less
frequent, but when it occurred here, the explanation rarely
said so.

Several limits apply. Ten flips are too few to estimate an
acknowledgement rate, so we report counts only. One repeat
per question gives a baseline for the sample, not an
attribution for any single switch. We rate visible
explanations, not hidden reasoning, so a \emph{none} label
records what the text omits, not what the model used. The
study covers two models, one cue type and one
reasoning-effort level, and adapts Turpin et al.'s setup
rather than replicating it. Finally, the questions come from
public data~\cite{turpin2023code} and may appear in both
models' training data, and Original accuracy of 90.0\% and
96.7\% leaves little room for the cue to lower it.
